There is something profoundly disturbing about human beings.

<EssaySoundtrack />

We can spend years praying for something, crying over its absence, promising ourselves that everything will be different once we finally receive it.

And then, when it arrives, we forget the person who was praying.

We ask God for success, and when we become successful, we treat other people as though they are beneath us.

We ask for love, and when someone finally loves us, we become careless with their heart.

We ask for freedom, and when we obtain it, we begin controlling the people around us.

We ask for opportunities, and when doors finally open, we start looking down on those still standing outside.

We ask for peace, then fill our lives with things that destroy it.

And somehow, we convince ourselves that because we have achieved what we wanted, we must have become better people along the way.

**What a beautiful lie.**

I have been thinking about a sentence that makes me deeply uncomfortable.

*Don't celebrate in sin what you achieved through prayer.*

Not because I believe every achievement is a divine reward or every failure is a punishment.

I don't.

And certainly not because I consider myself qualified to judge the moral condition of everybody else's life.

But because I think the sentence exposes a contradiction that exists even beyond religion.

**How can we spend our lives asking for something good, only to become careless with goodness once we possess it?**

How can we ask to be loved and then treat love as an entitlement?

How can we demand respect and then humiliate people?

How can we speak endlessly about gratitude while behaving as though the world owes us everything?

Perhaps the greatest test of character isn't what we do when we have nothing.

Perhaps it's what we do when we finally get everything we once wanted.

## The person you were when you had nothing.

I want you to remember a time when you desperately wanted something.

A job.

A relationship.

An opportunity.

An answer.

A way out.

Perhaps you prayed.

Perhaps you didn't believe in God, but still found yourself looking toward the ceiling in the middle of the night, hoping that somehow things would change.

Perhaps you promised yourself that if you ever got another chance, you would never take it for granted.

You would be kinder.

More patient.

More generous.

More appreciative.

You would remember what it felt like to have nothing.

And then, eventually, something changed.

You received the opportunity.

Someone believed in you.

A door opened.

The uncertainty began to disappear.

And slowly, almost without noticing, the extraordinary became ordinary.

The thing you once desperately wanted became something you complained about.

The person you once feared losing became someone whose presence you stopped appreciating.

The opportunity you once considered impossible became something you felt entitled to.

And the life you once imagined with tears in your eyes became the life you were too distracted to enjoy.

I don't think this makes us evil.

I think it makes us human.

But I also think we should be terrified of how easily gratitude turns into familiarity, and familiarity into entitlement.

Because there is something tragic about receiving an answer to a prayer and eventually forgetting why you were praying.

**The blessing didn't necessarily change. Your relationship with it did.**

And perhaps that's why gratitude cannot simply be an emotion we experience when something good happens.

It has to become something we practice when the excitement disappears.

## What if the things you most wish had never happened are part of what made you human?

I once came across an interview in which a man was asked about learning to love the very things he most wished had never happened.

He spoke about existence as a gift.

And with existence, suffering.

He suggested that if we are going to be grateful for life, perhaps we must confront the fact that suffering is inseparable from living.

Then came a question that has stayed with me.

*What punishments of God are not gifts?*

I find that question extraordinary.

And I also find it dangerous.

Because there are at least two ways to understand it.

The first is beautiful.

Perhaps experiences we never would have chosen can eventually deepen our understanding of other people's pain.

Perhaps grief can make us more compassionate.

Perhaps loss can teach us to recognize the fragility of things we previously took for granted.

Perhaps the things we desperately wish had never happened can become part of the reason we're capable of understanding somebody else's suffering.

But the second interpretation troubles me.

Because if we begin describing every painful experience as a gift, we risk pretending that suffering is automatically good.

And it isn't.

A child who loses a parent has not received a prize.

A person experiencing abuse has not been given a spiritual opportunity they should be grateful for.

Someone living through war does not owe the world a beautiful explanation of their suffering.

I cannot accept that kind of reasoning.

**There is a difference between finding meaning after suffering and claiming that suffering was necessary for meaning to exist.**

You may eventually become more compassionate because of something terrible.

That doesn't make the terrible thing good.

You may learn something valuable after losing someone you love.

That doesn't make their death desirable.

You may rebuild your life after everything collapses.

That doesn't mean the collapse was a blessing in disguise.

Sometimes the most honest response to suffering is simply to acknowledge that something awful happened.

And still believe that goodness is possible afterward.

For me, gratitude does not have to mean celebrating every event that brought me to where I am.

It can mean refusing to let those events take away my ability to appreciate what remains.

**I can be grateful that I survived without being grateful for everything I had to survive.**

And I think that distinction matters.

## The problem with people who have suffered is that we sometimes believe it makes us right.

There is a strange kind of arrogance that can grow out of suffering.

We begin believing that because we've experienced pain, we understand life better than everyone else.

Because we've lost something, we know what truly matters.

Because we've endured hardship, our opinions deserve greater respect.

Because we've struggled, people who haven't struggled in the same way must somehow be less capable of understanding the world.

And perhaps sometimes our experiences really do give us insights that others don't possess.

But experience and wisdom are not the same thing.

A person can endure extraordinary hardship and learn almost nothing about kindness.

Someone can survive a terrible childhood and still become a terrible parent.

Someone can experience injustice and still treat others unjustly.

And someone can spend their entire life suffering while remaining unable to recognize suffering in anybody else.

I think that might be one of the saddest things about human nature.

We know exactly how much our own pain hurts.

Yet we sometimes behave as though other people's pain needs to be justified before it deserves our attention.

*You think your life is difficult?*

*You should see what I've been through.*

*You think you're exhausted?*

*I've worked twice as hard.*

*You think you're suffering?*

*You don't know what suffering means.*

What a ridiculous competition.

As though pain were a sport.

As though the person who has suffered the most automatically wins the right to dismiss everybody else.

I don't want to live that way.

And I don't want suffering to turn me into that kind of person.

If anything, I hope that the difficulties I experience will make it easier for me to recognize the difficulties of others.

Not because our experiences are identical.

But because suffering should remind us that everybody carries something we cannot fully see.

**If your pain has made you incapable of caring about somebody else's, then perhaps you have survived the experience without learning the lesson you claim it taught you.**

## We have forgotten how to let people be proud of who they are.

There's something else I want to discuss.

And I know it might make people uncomfortable.

Good.

Because I think we've become remarkably bad at having honest conversations about men and women without turning everything into a competition.

Imagine asking a little girl what she loves about being a girl.

She tells you she's strong.

Beautiful.

Intelligent.

Capable.

Wonderful.

Celebrate that.

Encourage her.

Let her discover her own worth without teaching her that she needs permission to believe in herself.

Now imagine asking a little boy the same question.

He says he's strong.

Intelligent.

Brave.

Proud of being a boy.

Why should that automatically be understood as a statement about girls?

Why must pride in one identity be interpreted as hostility toward another?

Of course girls can be strong.

Of course boys can be intelligent.

Those things were never mutually exclusive.

And recognizing the qualities of one person does not require denying the qualities of another.

I think we need to become much better at understanding that.

For generations, women have faced discrimination, exclusion, and restrictions on their freedom.

Many of those inequalities continue to exist.

Recognizing that reality matters.

But acknowledging the suffering of women should not require dismissing the struggles of men.

And recognizing the isolation or frustration experienced by some men should never become an excuse to diminish women's rights.

**Human dignity is not a competition in which somebody must lose for somebody else to win.**

Yet we keep approaching it that way.

We tell boys they should be strong, but sometimes make them feel ashamed of admitting they're frightened.

We tell them to be emotionally available, but still encounter environments in which vulnerability is mocked.

We tell them to take responsibility, which is important, but don't always teach them how to ask for help when responsibility becomes overwhelming.

And then we wonder why some men feel isolated, directionless, or unable to express what they're experiencing.

Some respond by withdrawing.

Some become angry.

Some search for communities that promise them superiority instead of helping them develop self-respect.

And that's where another danger begins.

Because loneliness can make people vulnerable to anybody who promises to explain why the world is against them.

But I refuse to believe the solution is teaching men to hate women.

Just as I refuse to believe that empowering women requires teaching boys to apologize for being boys.

We need something better.

We need to raise people who are comfortable with their own dignity without feeling threatened by somebody else's.

Men who can be strong without being cruel.

Women who can be ambitious without having to justify their existence.

Boys who can be vulnerable without feeling ashamed.

Girls who can be confident without being told to make themselves smaller.

And people who understand that equality is not the destruction of difference.

It is the recognition that difference does not determine human worth.

**A boy should never have to become ashamed of himself for a girl to feel empowered. And a girl should never have to become smaller for a boy to feel strong.**

I don't understand why that should be controversial.

## There is a difference between believing in yourself and believing the universe owes you something.

Let's talk about the so-called Law of Attraction.

You've probably heard the idea.

Think positively.

Visualize success.

Believe that something is already yours.

And eventually, the universe will bring it into your life.

People describe thoughts as magnets.

They suggest that if you concentrate on success, you will attract success.

If you concentrate on failure, you will attract failure.

And if you think about something often enough, reality will somehow organize itself around your desires.

I understand why people find this attractive.

Who wouldn't want to believe that the universe is secretly working in their favour?

But there is a problem.

We have no convincing scientific evidence that our thoughts magnetically attract external events in the way these claims suggest.

And when taken too far, the idea becomes cruel.

Did somebody become ill because they failed to think positively?

Did a person lose their home because their thoughts attracted financial hardship?

Did a child born into war somehow manifest the circumstances of their birth?

Of course not.

And I think we should be extremely careful with any philosophy that makes people personally responsible for suffering they did not choose.

But here's the interesting part.

**Your thoughts may not control the universe, but they can influence how you move through it.**

And that is powerful enough.

If you wake up every morning convinced that everybody is against you, you may begin interpreting ambiguous situations as evidence of hostility.

If you believe that every rejection proves you're incapable of succeeding, you may stop attempting things that could have changed your life.

If you constantly focus on everything that might go wrong, you may become so afraid of failure that you never give yourself the opportunity to discover what could go right.

But if you begin asking different questions, your attention may change.

You start noticing opportunities.

You become more willing to attempt difficult things.

You recognize progress that previously seemed insignificant.

You begin responding to situations with greater confidence.

Not because the universe has decided to reward your thoughts.

But because your thoughts influence your attention, and your attention can influence your decisions.

And your decisions, repeated over time, can change your life.

That's not magic.

That's being human.

**You cannot think your way into every opportunity. But you can think yourself out of opportunities you haven't even tried to pursue.**

And there's a difference.

## Tell your mind what matters. Then give your life a reason to believe you.

I came across another idea that I find surprisingly practical.

When you wake up in the morning, instead of immediately asking what might go wrong, ask yourself what could make today a good day.

Not a perfect day.

Not a day without difficulties.

A good day.

And when you go to sleep, ask yourself what went well.

What did you learn?

What did somebody do for you?

What did you accomplish?

What made you smile?

What did you handle better than you might have handled it before?

These questions won't magically change your circumstances.

But they may change what you notice.

And I think there's a great deal of value in that.

Because our minds can become extraordinarily skilled at finding evidence for whatever story we've already decided is true.

If we believe our lives are miserable, we notice the things that make us miserable.

If we believe we're failures, every setback becomes another piece of evidence.

If we believe nobody cares about us, we may overlook the people who quietly demonstrate that they do.

And this can happen even when there are genuinely good things present.

But I want to be careful.

Because sometimes things really are terrible.

Sometimes the problem isn't perspective.

It's poverty.

It's grief.

It's discrimination.

It's illness.

It's being treated badly.

And those things cannot be solved by telling somebody to smile in the mirror.

A person struggling to pay rent doesn't need a lecture about gratitude instead of financial support.

Someone experiencing depression doesn't need to be told that their suffering is simply the result of negative thoughts.

And someone facing injustice doesn't need to be convinced that the problem disappears if they interpret it differently.

**A better perspective is not a substitute for a better reality.**

But when we do have room to choose where we direct our attention, I think we should take that responsibility seriously.

Because it is possible to be aware of everything wrong with the world without becoming incapable of recognizing anything beautiful within it.

And perhaps that balance is one of the most difficult things to achieve.

To remain critical without becoming cynical.

Hopeful without becoming naive.

Ambitious without becoming entitled.

Grateful without becoming indifferent.

## The most dangerous prayer is the one that becomes an excuse.

I want to return to where we began.

Prayer.

And the relationship between what we ask for and what we do after receiving it.

Because there is something that troubles me about the way we sometimes use faith.

We pray for success.

But when success comes, we use it to justify arrogance.

We pray for a meaningful relationship.

But when we find someone who loves us, we begin treating their loyalty as something we're owed.

We pray for strength.

But when we become stronger, we use our strength to intimidate people.

We pray for freedom.

But when we're finally free, we stop caring about those who aren't.

We pray for forgiveness.

But refuse to forgive anybody who has wronged us.

We ask God to understand our mistakes.

Then condemn other people for theirs.

And if somebody points out the contradiction, we become defensive.

Because it's much easier to use religion to explain why we're right than to allow it to challenge the way we're living.

I don't think faith should make us feel morally superior.

If anything, I think genuine faith should make us more aware of how much we still need to learn.

How easily we become selfish.

How quickly we justify our mistakes.

How often we demand compassion from others while forgetting to offer it ourselves.

And how much damage we can cause while remaining completely convinced that our intentions are good.

**The purpose of prayer cannot simply be to obtain the life we want. It must also challenge the kind of person we are becoming while pursuing it.**

Otherwise, what have we really learned?

We have acquired things.

But have we become better?

We have received opportunities.

But have we become more generous?

We have experienced love.

But have we become more loving?

We have survived suffering.

But have we become more compassionate?

Those are the questions I find important.

Not how loudly somebody declares their faith.

Not how publicly they celebrate their blessings.

Not how confidently they claim that God is on their side.

But how they treat the person who has nothing to offer them.

How they behave when nobody is watching.

How they respond to somebody who disagrees with them.

And whether they remember the dignity of others when their own ambitions are at stake.

Because it is entirely possible to speak beautifully about God while treating human beings terribly.

And I struggle to imagine a greater contradiction.

## What if gratitude is not happiness, but responsibility?

We've made gratitude sound like something comfortable.

Be thankful.

Appreciate what you have.

Count your blessings.

And there's nothing wrong with those ideas.

But I wonder whether gratitude should demand something more of us.

If you're grateful for your education, what do you do when somebody else is denied the opportunity to learn?

If you're grateful for your freedom, how do you respond when somebody else's freedom is restricted?

If you're grateful for the people who believed in you, do you make room for people who haven't yet been given a chance?

If you're grateful for being forgiven, how do you treat people who come to you seeking forgiveness?

If you're grateful for kindness, are you kind when kindness becomes inconvenient?

Because appreciation without responsibility can become little more than a pleasant feeling.

And I don't think gratitude is supposed to end with feeling good about ourselves.

I think it should change how we behave.

**If you truly understand the value of something, you should become more careful about how you treat people who don't have it.**

That doesn't mean you're responsible for solving every problem in the world.

You're not.

It doesn't mean you should feel guilty every time something good happens to you.

You shouldn't.

And it doesn't mean that happiness is somehow disrespectful toward people who are suffering.

But I think there is a difference between appreciating your own circumstances and becoming indifferent to everybody else's.

And the world desperately needs more people who understand that distinction.

## Maybe the greatest achievement is not getting what you prayed for.

Imagine reaching a point in your life where you have everything you once wanted.

The career.

The financial security.

The person you love.

The freedom to decide what happens next.

Imagine looking around and realizing that the things you once begged for have become your everyday reality.

What would you feel?

Pride?

Relief?

Gratitude?

Perhaps all three.

But let me ask you something more difficult.

Would the people around you still recognize the person you promised yourself you would become?

Would your success have made you kinder?

Would your security have made you more generous?

Would your freedom have made you more understanding of other people's struggles?

Would your experience of suffering have made you more compassionate?

Or would you have become somebody who believes that because you have suffered, you are now entitled to make other people suffer?

Because I can imagine something worse than never receiving what you prayed for.

**Receiving everything you wanted and becoming someone who no longer understands why you wanted it.**

Imagine spending years praying for love, only to become the reason somebody else stops believing in it.

Imagine praying for an opportunity, only to close the door behind you so nobody else can follow.

Imagine praying for the strength to overcome cruelty, only to become cruel when you finally acquire power.

What a tragedy.

To survive the things that once made you feel powerless and then recreate them for somebody else.

To spend years asking for kindness and eventually become incapable of giving it.

To receive what you called a blessing and use it as permission to abandon the principles you once claimed to value.

I don't want that.

And I don't want to spend my life chasing achievements that slowly take away the qualities that made those achievements meaningful.

## There will come a day when none of your achievements can answer for your character.

I think about death more often than I probably should.

Not because I want it.

But because there is something clarifying about remembering that everything we possess is temporary.

One day, the titles disappear.

The money belongs to someone else.

The photographs become old.

The arguments we were so desperate to win become irrelevant.

The people whose approval we spent years chasing eventually stop thinking about us.

And all the things we believed made us important begin losing their importance.

I wonder what will matter then.

Not how many people admired me.

But whether the people who knew me felt respected.

Not whether I became the most successful person in a room.

But whether I made the people in that room feel smaller simply because I could.

Not whether I won every argument.

But whether I had the humility to admit when I was wrong.

Not whether I endured more suffering than everybody else.

But whether I remained capable of recognizing theirs.

Because at some point, I think life asks us to confront the difference between what we achieved and what we became.

And we might discover that the two are not nearly as connected as we imagined.

You can achieve extraordinary things and remain a terrible human being.

You can achieve very little by society's standards and live with extraordinary dignity.

You can be celebrated publicly and feared privately.

You can be admired by strangers and deeply hurt the people closest to you.

And you can spend your entire life convincing people that you're good while quietly knowing that you've stopped living according to the principles you once believed in.

**The world may celebrate what you accomplished. But your conscience must live with how you accomplished it.**

And perhaps that is the judgment we should be most concerned about.

Not the opinions of strangers.

Not the expectations of an audience.

But the quiet moment when there is nobody left to impress, and we have to confront the truth about ourselves.

## So what are you actually praying for?

I want you to ask yourself something.

And this time, don't give yourself the beautiful answer.

Give yourself the honest one.

When you ask for success, do you want the opportunity to create something meaningful?

Or do you want to become important enough that nobody can question you?

When you ask for love, do you want to understand another person?

Or do you want somebody who will never challenge the version of yourself you've constructed?

When you ask for strength, do you want the courage to do what is right?

Or do you want the power to ensure nobody can ever hurt your pride again?

When you ask for wealth, do you want security and independence?

Or do you want the ability to prove that you're worth more than people who once doubted you?

When you ask for recognition, do you want your work to matter?

Or do you want other people to finally tell you that you matter?

These are uncomfortable questions.

I know.

Because they're uncomfortable for me, too.

And perhaps we don't always know our own answers.

Perhaps our motivations are mixtures of generosity and ego, ambition and insecurity, love and fear.

Perhaps that's simply part of being human.

But I think we should at least have the courage to examine them.

Because if we don't, we may spend our entire lives praying for things that eventually destroy the qualities we were trying to develop.

And then we'll wonder how everything went wrong.

## Don't celebrate in sin what you achieved through prayer.

I keep returning to that sentence.

And the more I think about it, the less I understand it as a warning against enjoying life.

I don't think goodness requires misery.

I don't think faith should make us ashamed of happiness.

I don't think celebrating an achievement is wrong.

Enjoy your success.

Love the people who love you.

Travel.

Laugh.

Build.

Create.

Dance if you want to.

Be proud of the things you've worked for.

Appreciate the extraordinary privilege of being alive.

But do not use your happiness as an excuse for cruelty.

Do not use your success as permission for arrogance.

Do not use your freedom to deny somebody else's.

Do not use your suffering to justify hurting people.

Do not use your faith to escape responsibility.

And do not mistake receiving what you wanted for proof that every decision you made along the way was right.

Because the fact that something worked does not mean it was good.

The fact that you won does not mean you were just.

The fact that you're successful does not mean you're wise.

And the fact that you're grateful does not mean you're incapable of causing harm.

We must learn to hold these contradictions within ourselves.

To acknowledge our strengths without becoming arrogant.

To recognize our weaknesses without becoming ashamed of existing.

To be grateful without pretending suffering is beautiful.

To believe in ourselves without believing that the universe owes us a reward.

And to pursue extraordinary things without losing our capacity for ordinary human kindness.

I don't know whether everything happens for a reason.

I don't know whether every unanswered prayer is protecting us from something we cannot see.

I don't know whether every loss will eventually lead somewhere better.

And I refuse to pretend that I do.

But I know that when good things happen, we have choices about how we respond to them.

We can become more entitled.

Or more appreciative.

More arrogant.

Or more humble.

More indifferent.

Or more compassionate.

And when life becomes difficult, we can allow our pain to become an excuse for cruelty.

Or we can try, however imperfectly, to prevent the suffering we've experienced from becoming suffering we inflict on somebody else.

Perhaps that's what I want to remember.

That success is not salvation.

That suffering is not holiness.

That strength is not goodness.

That gratitude is not the absence of pain.

And that faith, whatever form it takes in our lives, should never become an excuse to stop questioning our own behavior.

So when you finally receive the thing you once begged for, I hope you celebrate.

I hope you feel the happiness you imagined.

I hope you experience the relief of realizing that something you feared would never happen has finally become real.

But I hope you remember the person who was asking.

The promises you made.

The compassion you wanted to receive.

The dignity you wished others would recognize in you.

And the kind of human being you hoped the experience would help you become.

Because perhaps the greatest tragedy is not that our prayers go unanswered.

**Perhaps it's that some of them are answered, and we forget how to live with gratitude once they are.**

And perhaps the greatest achievement is not receiving everything we asked for.

Perhaps it is arriving at the other side of our ambitions, our suffering, our success, and our mistakes, and discovering that we have not abandoned the goodness we once prayed to find.

So ask for the opportunity.

Ask for love.

Ask for courage.

Ask for freedom.

Ask for the strength to continue when life becomes difficult.

But remember to ask for something else.

Something that might be far more important than everything you've spent your life pursuing.

**Ask for the wisdom not to become the very person you once prayed to be protected from.**

Because what good is receiving everything you ever wanted if, in the process, you lose everything that made you worthy of enjoying it?

*Samir Seddiqi*

*4 November 2026*
