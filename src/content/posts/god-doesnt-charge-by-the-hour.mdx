I was reading the Bible recently when I came across something that made me stop.

Matthew 7:6.

**"Don't throw your pearls to pigs."**

What an extraordinary sentence.

Not particularly gentle.

Not especially comforting.

And certainly not the kind of thing you'd expect from the sanitized version of Christianity that people sometimes present today.

There it was.

A warning about giving something precious to those who will neither understand nor respect it.

And the more I thought about it, the more I realized how much of my life I've spent doing exactly that.

Giving explanations to people who were never interested in understanding.

Giving loyalty to people who considered loyalty a weakness.

Giving second chances to people who treated forgiveness as permission.

Giving time to people who only remembered my existence when they needed something.

And then wondering why I felt exhausted.

But something else came to mind.

A sentence I heard recently that made me laugh before it made me uncomfortable.

**"The difference between tax fraud and wealth management is just the hourly rate of your lawyer."**

Now, before somebody starts writing an angry email, no, those are not legally the same thing.

Tax fraud is illegal. Legitimate wealth management is not.

But the joke reveals something we don't particularly enjoy discussing.

Society has become extraordinarily sophisticated at finding respectable names for things.

And sometimes, the more money you have, the more respectable the vocabulary becomes.

A poor person struggles with debt.

A corporation undergoes financial restructuring.

An ordinary person tries to avoid paying what they owe.

A wealthy client hires a team to identify every lawful way of reducing their liabilities.

A struggling person is irresponsible.

A successful person is strategic.

Of course, these situations are not necessarily identical.

But isn't it remarkable how quickly our moral language changes depending on who we're talking about?

And isn't it even more remarkable how easily we accept it?

**We've built a world where almost everything has a price, including the explanations people use to justify themselves.**

## Stop explaining your worth to people committed to misunderstanding it.

Let's return to the Bible.

I think many people read the passage about pearls and immediately imagine the people who have disappointed them.

Their ex.

Their former friend.

Someone who betrayed their trust.

Someone who never appreciated their sacrifices.

And perhaps there's a lesson there.

But I want to be careful about something.

The passage is not a license to declare everyone who disagrees with us unworthy.

That's exactly the kind of arrogance that religious people should be especially cautious about.

Because earlier in the same chapter, Jesus warns against judging others while ignoring our own faults.

That's the part we conveniently forget.

We love being told to protect our pearls.

We're less enthusiastic about examining whether we've been trampling somebody else's.

And perhaps that's where this becomes interesting.

**Everybody thinks they're the one holding the pearls. Nobody wants to consider that they might be the one standing in the mud.**

We all know someone who refuses to listen.

Someone who believes they're always right.

Someone who confuses confidence with intelligence.

Someone who demands respect while offering none.

But what if, occasionally, that person is us?

What if we're the ones dismissing somebody else's sincerity?

What if we're the ones treating another person's affection as something ordinary because we know they'll keep giving it?

What if we're the ones who only recognize value after it disappears?

It's easy to talk about protecting yourself from people who don't appreciate you.

It's considerably harder to ask whether you've appreciated the people who trusted you.

And I think religion becomes dangerous when we use it exclusively to diagnose what's wrong with everybody else.

## Not everything that is valuable can be measured.

I find it fascinating that we live in a society obsessed with value but remarkably confused about what value actually means.

We know the price of a hotel room.

We know the price of a car.

We know the price of an expensive watch.

We can calculate someone's salary, estimate the value of their company, and judge their professional importance by their title.

But ask somebody what loyalty is worth.

What is the value of a person who stays when you have nothing impressive to offer?

What is the value of a friend who tells you the truth when everybody else is applauding your mistakes?

What is the value of somebody who believes in you before there's any evidence that believing in you will benefit them?

Suddenly, our sophisticated understanding of value becomes rather useless.

And here's the tragedy.

We sometimes treat the things we can buy as precious and the things we receive freely as disposable.

A person spends thousands on something they can replace tomorrow.

Then loses a friendship they may never be able to rebuild.

Someone protects their reputation more carefully than their character.

Someone will spend a fortune defending their public image but refuses to spend five minutes admitting they were wrong.

We call that success.

I call it a very expensive form of stupidity.

**If you know the price of everything but cannot recognize the value of sincerity, you're not wealthy. You're simply expensive to maintain.**

## The most respectable word in the dictionary is apparently "strategy."

Let's talk about money.

Because I've noticed something extraordinary.

We have a language for ordinary people's mistakes and a completely different language for sophisticated people's decisions.

When someone with very little money makes a financial mistake, we tend to moralize it.

They should have planned better.

They should have worked harder.

They should have been more responsible.

They should have understood the consequences.

And sometimes that's fair.

People should take responsibility for their decisions.

But when a wealthy individual or a powerful corporation uses complex financial structures to reduce tax obligations, the conversation often becomes more technical.

Efficiency.

Optimization.

Planning.

Strategy.

Risk management.

Again, let me be precise.

Using lawful tax deductions is not fraud.

Paying a competent accountant is not corruption.

And nobody is automatically unethical because they've become wealthy.

But there is a real distinction between what the law permits and what a person can reasonably defend as fair.

Some tax arrangements follow the intended purpose of the rules.

Others are designed to exploit gaps or inconsistencies between them.

And some cross the legal line altogether.

The problem is that evaluating these arrangements can require expertise, time, information, and professional advice that not everybody can afford.

That's where the original joke becomes uncomfortable.

Not because a lawyer can magically turn a crime into something legal.

They cannot.

But because wealth can buy access to complex legal and financial options that ordinary people may never even know exist.

**The law may be written for everyone. Understanding every opportunity within it is considerably more expensive.**

And I wonder why we don't discuss that contradiction more often.

We're perfectly comfortable telling people that ignorance of the law is no excuse.

Yet we have created legal and financial systems complicated enough that entire professions exist to interpret them.

Think about that.

You are expected to understand your obligations.

But understanding all your options may require paying somebody whose hourly rate exceeds what another person earns in several days.

And somehow, we're supposed to pretend that access doesn't matter.

## Being legal doesn't automatically make you good.

This is where I think we need to stop confusing morality with compliance.

Something can be legal and still be cruel.

Something can be permitted and still be exploitative.

Something can be contractually acceptable and still demonstrate a complete absence of decency.

The law establishes enforceable boundaries.

It does not provide a complete definition of human goodness.

You can treat an employee terribly while remaining within the technical limits of a contract.

You can use somebody's desperation to negotiate terms they would never have accepted under better circumstances.

You can tell a person a technically accurate statement while deliberately creating a false impression.

You can exploit someone's trust without necessarily committing a crime.

And then, when somebody questions your behavior, you can proudly announce:

"But I didn't break any laws."

Congratulations.

You have successfully established that you weren't prosecuted for being an asshole.

What an extraordinary moral achievement.

I don't believe legality is irrelevant.

It's essential.

But I refuse to believe it should be the highest standard we set for ourselves.

**If your strongest defense of your behavior is that nobody can legally stop you, perhaps you should spend less time consulting your lawyer and more time examining your conscience.**

And this applies to everybody.

Not just rich people.

Not just companies.

Not just institutions.

All of us.

Because we are remarkably creative when it comes to finding reasons why the rules of decency should not apply to our particular situation.

## We have become experts at defending ourselves and amateurs at understanding others.

There is something almost comical about the way we judge behavior.

When somebody else lies, they're dishonest.

When we lie, the situation was complicated.

When somebody else is selfish, they lack character.

When we're selfish, we're protecting our boundaries.

When somebody else abandons a commitment, they're unreliable.

When we do it, we're prioritizing our mental health.

When somebody else makes a bad decision, we question their intelligence.

When we make one, we explain the circumstances.

We judge other people by what they did.

We judge ourselves by why we did it.

And then we wonder why everybody believes they're a good person.

Perhaps that's the greatest advantage of being human.

We're capable of turning almost anything into an explanation that makes us look reasonable.

But being able to explain your behavior doesn't automatically justify it.

And being intelligent enough to defend a decision doesn't mean the decision was wise.

**Sometimes the most dangerous person in the room is not the one who doesn't understand morality. It's the one who's intelligent enough to explain why morality doesn't apply to them.**

That person can be wealthy.

Poor.

Religious.

Atheist.

Educated.

Uneducated.

It doesn't matter.

Self-deception has never required a particular bank balance.

## Don't confuse kindness with unlimited access.

Now here's the part of Matthew 7:6 that feels especially personal to me.

I used to think being a good person meant always being available.

Always explaining.

Always understanding.

Always giving people another opportunity.

And I still believe in kindness.

I still believe in forgiveness.

I still believe people can change.

But I no longer think those beliefs require me to abandon every boundary I have.

You can forgive someone without trusting them again.

You can understand someone's behavior without accepting it.

You can care about somebody without allowing them to repeatedly hurt you.

You can wish a person well and still decide they should no longer have access to your life.

And you do not have to turn every departure into a courtroom where you must present evidence proving your right to leave.

Sometimes the explanation has already been given.

Sometimes the conversation has already happened.

Sometimes you've spent so long trying to be understood that you've forgotten to ask whether the other person has made any effort to understand you.

**There is no virtue in repeatedly sacrificing your dignity to prove that you are kind.**

But there's another side.

Don't become so obsessed with protecting yourself that you start treating everyone as a threat.

Don't convince yourself that being disappointed once means nobody deserves your trust.

And don't use one Bible verse to justify becoming arrogant, indifferent, or cruel.

A boundary should protect your ability to love.

Not destroy it.

## The world doesn't need more impressive people. It needs fewer hypocrites.

I've been thinking about what we actually admire.

We admire confidence.

Ambition.

Money.

Influence.

Intelligence.

The ability to win.

The ability to convince others.

But I wonder whether we've become too impressed by competence and not nearly impressed enough by character.

Someone makes millions, and we immediately assume they understand life.

Someone builds an enormous audience, and we assume their opinions deserve attention.

Someone wears an expensive suit, speaks confidently, and uses sufficiently complicated vocabulary, and suddenly we're willing to call almost anything they do brilliant.

Perhaps it is.

But perhaps they're simply good at appearing successful.

Being powerful does not make somebody principled.

Being wealthy does not make somebody wise.

Being religious does not make somebody compassionate.

And being well educated does not make somebody honest.

I want us to stop confusing these things.

A successful person can be profoundly dishonest.

A poor person can possess extraordinary integrity.

A religious leader can fail to live according to the values they preach.

And someone who has never opened a Bible can demonstrate remarkable compassion.

If your understanding of morality depends entirely on somebody's appearance, position, or reputation, you aren't evaluating character.

You're evaluating packaging.

**We've become so obsessed with how impressive people look that we've almost forgotten to ask what happens to others when those people get what they want.**

And perhaps that's the question we should be asking more often.

Not what somebody has achieved.

But what they were willing to do to achieve it.

## Be careful. The person trampling the pearls might be you.

I want to return to the passage one final time.

Because it would be easy to end this article by telling you to protect your energy, remove toxic people, and stop wasting your time on those who don't appreciate you.

And perhaps you should do some of those things.

But I think that would be too convenient.

Too comfortable.

Too easy.

Because everybody reading this already has someone in mind who disappointed them.

Someone who didn't deserve their loyalty.

Someone who failed to recognize their value.

Someone who treated their kindness carelessly.

But I want to ask a different question.

**Whose pearls have you been trampling?**

Who gave you their time when you barely acknowledged it?

Who trusted you when you hadn't earned that trust?

Who offered you kindness that you treated as an obligation?

Who told you something deeply personal, only for you to use it against them later?

Who believed in you before you became successful?

And have you remembered them now that you no longer need their help?

Because perhaps this is the uncomfortable truth.

We are not always the innocent person in the story.

Sometimes we're the one who didn't appreciate what we received.

Sometimes we're the one who took advantage of somebody else's patience.

Sometimes we're the one who used impressive language to disguise an ugly intention.

Sometimes we're the one who demanded morality from everybody else while quietly negotiating exceptions for ourselves.

And until we're willing to acknowledge that possibility, we're not particularly interested in becoming better people.

We're just interested in feeling superior.

## God doesn't charge by the hour.

I keep thinking about that joke.

Tax fraud.

Wealth management.

The hourly rate of a lawyer.

It's funny because it exaggerates a real discomfort with inequality, access, and the respectable language surrounding money.

But I think the deeper problem goes beyond taxes.

We've become extraordinarily skilled at purchasing explanations.

We pay people to improve our public image.

To defend our decisions.

To negotiate the consequences.

To make our intentions sound sophisticated.

And sometimes that's completely legitimate.

Everybody deserves competent advice and legal representation.

But there is something no amount of professional expertise should be able to purchase.

A clean conscience.

You cannot hire somebody to become a decent human being on your behalf.

You cannot outsource integrity.

You cannot pay a consultant to make cruelty compassionate.

You cannot turn betrayal into loyalty simply because you've found a more flattering word for it.

And perhaps that's where my faith becomes uncomfortable.

Because I don't believe God is particularly impressed by expensive vocabulary.

I don't think a beautiful explanation changes the moral consequences of something ugly.

I don't think success automatically proves virtue.

And I certainly don't think wealth makes somebody more deserving of dignity than anybody else.

But I also don't believe poverty automatically makes somebody morally superior.

Character cannot be determined by a bank statement in either direction.

The question is much more difficult than that.

What do you do when you're powerful enough to get away with something?

How do you treat somebody who cannot benefit you?

What happens to your principles when following them becomes expensive?

And when nobody is watching, do you still believe in the values you so confidently defend in public?

Those questions cannot be answered by your accountant.

Or your lawyer.

Or your followers.

Or the people who depend on keeping you happy.

Eventually, you have to answer them yourself.

So protect what is precious.

Stop giving unlimited access to people who repeatedly demonstrate that they will misuse it.

Stop confusing cruelty with strength.

Stop mistaking legal sophistication for moral superiority.

And stop treating wealth as evidence that somebody's behavior deserves a different standard of judgment.

But while you're doing all of that, remember something else.

**You are not automatically the pearl.**

You are not automatically the victim.

You are not automatically the righteous person standing above everybody else's mistakes.

Sometimes you are the one who needs to apologize.

Sometimes you're the one who needs to listen.

Sometimes you're the one who's been so busy defending yourself that you've forgotten to recognize the damage you've caused.

And sometimes the most valuable thing you can do is stop looking for somebody else to blame.

Because I don't want a world where morality is simply another luxury available to people who can afford to redefine it.

And I don't want a faith that exists only to tell me how terrible other people are.

I want something that challenges me, too.

Something that makes me uncomfortable when I become arrogant.

Something that reminds me that integrity matters most when nobody can force me to practice it.

Something that refuses to let me confuse winning with being right.

And perhaps that's what I find so powerful about that passage.

It makes me think about the things I should protect.

But it also forces me to consider the things other people have entrusted to me.

Their time.

Their trust.

Their dignity.

Their affection.

Their belief that I would treat them fairly.

Those are pearls, too.

And maybe the real test of character isn't simply knowing when to stop giving them away.

**Maybe it's knowing what to do when somebody places theirs in your hands.**

Because a pearl doesn't stop being precious just because somebody tramples it.

And a dishonest act doesn't become honorable just because somebody finds an expensive way to describe it.

**You can pay a lawyer to defend your actions.**

**You still have to live with the person who committed them.**

*Samir Seddiqi*

*9 November 2026*
