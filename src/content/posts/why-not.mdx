There is a particular kind of arrogance in telling a generation to be patient while asking it to accept less and less.

Study hard. Work harder. Pay the rent. Keep up with the bills. Be grateful you have a job. Be grateful you have a roof. Be grateful you have anything at all.

And when you finally ask whether life is supposed to be more than an endless exercise in endurance, someone tells you to grow up.

**No.**

Growing up should not mean learning to live without hope.

Look at the streets of Europe. People are coming together, raising their voices, questioning institutions, demanding to be heard. They do not all agree. Their reasons are not identical. But the sight of people refusing to become spectators in their own future means something to me.

Because the most dangerous sentence a society can teach its young people is not *you cannot change the world.*

It is *why bother trying?*

## Who taught us to expect so little?

When did a decent home become an unreasonable ambition?

When did education become something we praise in speeches while making the path toward it harder and harder?

When did politics become a conversation about the future conducted without the people who will have to live in it?

We are told to study so we can work. Work so we can pay. Pay so we can survive. Survive so we can wake up tomorrow and do it again.

**Somewhere along the way, the future became a bill.**

And then we are expected to smile and call this realism.

Well, **why the fuck not** demand something better?

Why not ask who benefits from the way things are arranged?

Why not question a decision simply because a minister signed it, a parliament passed it, or somebody important said it was inevitable?

A decision does not become wise because it is official. A system does not become just because it is familiar.

And young people do not become childish the moment they refuse to confuse resignation with maturity.

## To the people who refuse to disappear quietly

I want to tell you something.

**Thank you.**

Not because every slogan is right. Not because every protest has the answer. Not because the loudest person in a crowd should automatically be believed.

Thank you because you are reminding the rest of us that citizenship is not a five-minute act performed at a ballot box and then forgotten for years.

Democracy also lives in questions. In criticism. In organizing. In the courage to say, publicly and peacefully, *this is not good enough.*

You can disagree with a demonstration and still defend people's right to hold it. You can believe in institutions and still insist that institutions answer to the people they serve.

That should not be controversial.

It should be the beginning of civic life.

## I was born in a country where freedom is not guaranteed

I was born in Afghanistan.

Today I live in Europe. I study at a university. I work on building a company. I can speak, publish, challenge ideas, change my mind, and make mistakes.

**I am allowed to fail while chasing a dream.**

There are girls in the country where I was born who are denied the chance to walk into a secondary-school classroom.

I think about that often.

I do not mention Afghanistan to pretend that Europe's problems are the same. They are not. I mention it because I know how precious the freedom to question authority is, and how dangerous it becomes when people are persuaded that their voices do not matter.

Rights are not ornaments to display when times are easy.

They are promises that must hold when the questions get uncomfortable.

A society should not ask its citizens to be silent in order to prove that they love it.

**Sometimes the most faithful thing you can do for a place is demand that it live up to its own principles.**

## Anger is not the revolution. What we do with it is.

I am angry.

Angry that ambition so often feels like a luxury. Angry that people are told to lower their expectations before anyone seriously questions the decisions that shaped those expectations. Angry that we have learned to describe avoidable suffering as if it were weather.

But anger alone builds nothing.

And I will not pretend that smashing a window is the same as changing a law, or that frightening ordinary people is the same as freeing them.

If we want a future worth having, we need more than noise.

We need evidence. We need organization. We need the patience to build and the courage to confront. We need to hold leaders accountable even when they tell us what we want to hear.

We must refuse the easy temptation to blame our neighbours, migrants, minorities, or anyone else with less power than the people making the decisions.

**A movement that demands a voice for itself while trying to silence someone else has misunderstood freedom.**

The point is not to replace one kind of indifference with another.

The point is to make human dignity impossible to ignore.

## Call me naive. I can live with that.

I am twenty-two years old.

I have been told, in different ways, to be realistic. To wait. To accept that the world is complicated and that some things simply cannot change.

Of course the world is complicated.

That is not an argument for surrender.

Every institution we know was built by human beings. Every law was written by human beings. Every practice we call normal was once a choice, and choices can be examined, challenged, and changed.

There is no guarantee that a protest will succeed. No guarantee that a generation will agree on what comes next. No guarantee that the future will reward our courage.

But the absence of a guarantee is not the absence of a reason to try.

**Hope is not the belief that we will win. It is the refusal to let fear make every decision for us.**

So to the people standing in the streets, to the students asking difficult questions, to the workers demanding to be heard, to the people quietly organizing after everyone else has gone home:

I see you.

I do not need to agree with every word you say to respect your right to say it.

And I refuse to believe that the most we can offer the next generation is an instruction manual for surviving our mistakes.

## When they ask us what we did

One day, someone younger than us will ask what we did when we knew things could be better.

Did we listen?

Did we question the people making decisions in our name?

Did we defend the freedom of people we disagreed with?

Did we build anything that outlasted our anger?

Or did we call ourselves powerless because power was difficult to challenge?

I want to have an answer.

Not that we waited for a hero. Not that we confused posting with participating. Not that we mistook being cynical for being intelligent.

I want to say that we showed up. That we learned. That we argued without surrendering our humanity. That we built. That we failed, stood up, and kept going.

**None of us are free until all of us are free.**

Not just those born on the right side of a border. Not just those who can afford to wait. Not just those whose voices are already heard.

All of us.

So when somebody tells us that wanting a more dignified, more accountable, more human world is unrealistic, I have only one question left.

# WHY NOT?

*Samir Seddiqi*  
*19 October 2026*
